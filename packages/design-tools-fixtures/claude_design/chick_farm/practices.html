Practices · The honest version

How we farm, in unflattering detail.

Most farms list certifications. We're going to walk you through what actually happens, day to day, with the chickens and the land.

01 / Pasture rotation

Move the hens, not the manure.

We split our seven acres into five paddocks. Each flock spends 7 days on a paddock, then moves to the next. By the time they come back to the first paddock, 35 days have passed — long enough for grass to recover and for parasite cycles to break.

Mobile coops on skids make this easy. Joel built them out of welded steel and corrugated roofing, with nest boxes accessible from the outside. We pull them with a little Kubota tractor and the hens follow because that's where the food is.

02 / What they eat

Mostly bugs and grass. The rest is grain.

People are surprised by this, but a hen on real pasture gets the majority of her calories from foraging — grass, clover, beetles, worms, the occasional small frog. Grain is a supplement, not the main course.

Our grain comes from Green Mountain Mill in Brattleboro: an organic, non-GMO, soy-free blend of corn, peas, oats, flax, and a little kelp. We also feed kitchen scraps from two neighborhood restaurants — bread heels, vegetable trim, the occasional wedge of stale cheese.

03 / Heritage breeds

Slower, prettier, hardier.

The egg industry has spent 60 years breeding hens that lay 320 eggs a year and burn out at 18 months. We don't raise those.

Our flock is a mix: Plymouth Rocks, Buff Orpingtons, Welsummers, Marans, Ameraucanas, a handful of Easter Eggers. They lay 220–250 eggs a year, take longer to start, and live 5–7 years instead of 2. Their eggs are different colors, different sizes, and — importantly — produced by hens that aren't crashing through their own biology to get there.

04 / Egg handling

Collect, brush, candle, carton.

We collect twice a day, between 7–8 am and 3–4 pm. Eggs go straight into a dry basket — no washing. A fresh egg is coated in a natural protective layer (the "bloom") that keeps it shelf-stable. Washing strips it and forces refrigeration.

Back in the packing room: dry brush off any straw or speck, candle for cracks, weight-grade by hand, into the carton. Total time from nest to fridge: under twelve hours.

Industrial · what we don't do

  • Chlorine wash
  • Eggs up to 30 days old at carton
  • Mechanical sorting
  • Plastic carton
  • Eggs from 6+ farms blended

Here · what we do

  • Dry brush only
  • < 12 hours nest-to-fridge
  • Hand-graded
  • Pulp carton, returnable
  • Single farm, single flock
05 / Winter

Vermont winters are real. So are our hens.

Heritage breeds are cold-hardy. Their feet have countercurrent heat exchange (a thing). They tolerate temperatures down to about 0°F without supplemental heat. We don't heat the coops — heated coops cause more problems than they solve.

What we do: deep-pack bedding (composts in place, generates warmth), wind-break tarps on the coop's north wall, unfrozen water rotated three times a day. Egg production drops to about 35% in deep winter, which is fine and natural.

06 / End-of-life

The hard part, said plainly.

Hens slow down at age 4 or 5. The industrial approach is to "depopulate" the flock and start over — meat birds aren't economical at that point.

We don't do that. Our retired hens — about 60 currently — live in the smaller "retirement coop" near the orchard. They eat the same feed, lay the occasional egg, eat bugs, and die quietly of old age. It costs us money. We've decided we're okay with that.

07 / What we won't do

Worth being explicit about.

We'll never beak-trim. We'll never light the coops 16 hours a day to force off-season laying. We'll never replace the entire flock yearly. We'll never sell our name to a holding company. We'll never quietly ship in eggs from another farm to fill a shortage. We'll never raise more hens than we can know.

If any of this changes, you'll read it here first.
