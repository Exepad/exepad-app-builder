Our story · est. 2014

Six hens, one borrowed coop, and a Tuesday morning we still think about.

We didn't set out to start a farm. We just wanted better eggs. Twelve years and four hundred hens later, here's how it happened.

A short history

How we got here.

Twelve years compressed into nine moments. Most of the boring parts left out.

2014 · April
Six hens move in.
A pair of Plymouth Rocks, a Buff Orpington, and three Rhode Island Reds. Joel builds the coop out of pallets in a weekend.
2014 · June 17
First egg.
A small brown egg from the hen we called Pepper. We ate it standing over the kitchen sink. We still mark this date.
2016
The flock hits 60.
Friends start asking for eggs. We start charging $5/dozen, mostly to cover feed.
2018
We quit our day jobs.
Marta from teaching, Joel from carpentry. We buy the back seven acres from the Hendrick family next door.
2019
First market stall.
Putney Farmers Market, Wednesday mornings. We sell out by 9:30 the first day.
2021
Move to rotational pasture.
Five paddocks, mobile coops, weekly moves. The eggs taste different by August.
2023
412 hens.
We decide this is the size we want to be. Big enough to feed the valley, small enough to know each hen.
2025
Open Saturdays.
We start letting people walk the pasture and meet the hens. It turns out to be the best part of the week.
2026 · today

In Marta's words

Why we still do every collection by hand.

People ask why we don't automate more. There are conveyor systems now — the eggs roll out of the nest box, down a belt, into a sorter. We've seen them. They work fine.

The reason is simple, and a little stubborn: I want to walk through the coop twice a day. I want to see who's broody, who's molting, who's been sleeping in the wrong nest box, who's at the bottom of the pecking order this week. The eggs are how I know my flock is okay.

"The eggs are how I know my flock is okay."

It also means our eggs are never older than twelve hours when they go in the carton. They're never washed in chlorine because they don't need to be — collected fresh, they keep their natural cuticle, and that does the work of a wash.

Joel handles the building, the fencing, the feed runs, and the hard math of running a small business in a state that has gotten more expensive every single year. I handle the hens, the eggs, and the customers. Our daughter Wren handles whatever she feels like that day, which is usually rounding up the goslings.

That's the whole thing, really. We're not building an empire. We're not pivoting to private label. We are not, despite an actual offer in 2022, opening a second location.

We just want to keep making the best eggs we know how to make, for the people who'll come pick them up, for as long as our knees hold out. We hope you'll come visit.

— Marta & Joel
